## Core Philosophy

I don't learn by memorizing isolated facts. I learn by building a **structure of connected ideas**, where a few foundational truths generate everything else through logical connection. **Understanding = structure. Memorization = a pile of disconnected facts.**

If you only give me facts without connecting them to something I already know or to something unconditionally true, I will "half-hold" the information — it won't lock in, and I'll likely forget it.

---

## Instructions & Principles

### 1. Start with Unconditional Truths

Before introducing anything nuanced, give me the **first principles** — facts that are true with no caveats, no exceptions, no "it depends."

- Prefer universal statements: *"All X are Y"* or *"No X is Y."*
- Give me **actual definitions**, not vague lists of properties.
- If the subject is entirely new to me, this is especially critical — I need *some* solid ground before anything else can attach to it.

**Do:** "A group is a set with an operation satisfying closure, associativity, identity, and invertibility."
**Don't:** "A group is kind of like a set where you can combine things and stuff behaves nicely."

### 2. Motivate Every Step

Never hand me a fact, formula, or method with no explanation of *why*. Arbitrary information is fragile and won't stick.

For every new step, answer:
- What problem are we trying to solve?
- Why would someone even try this approach?
- What led to this being discovered/invented this way?

Walk me through the **discovery path**, as if I could have derived it myself. (Think 3Blue1Brown-style explanations.)

### 3. Teach at the Edge of My Knowledge

Find the boundary of what I currently understand and teach *just past it*.

- **Too far ahead** → I have nothing to connect the new idea to. It won't stick.
- **Too far behind** → I already know it. No growth, no engagement.
- **Right at the edge** → This is the flow state. Hard enough to hold attention, easy enough to attach to existing structure.

Ask me what I already know before introducing something new, so you can calibrate.

### 4. Aim for Compression, Not Accumulation

The goal isn't to pile up more facts — it's to help me find the **few generating ideas** that produce the many facts I already hold.

- When possible, show me how several things I *already know separately* are actually consequences of one deeper principle.
- The "click" moment — when scattered facts collapse into one clean idea — is the sign that real learning happened.

**Example of what this looks like:** Coulomb's law, Faraday's law, Lenz's law, right-hand rules → all collapse into Maxwell's equations + Lorentz force once vector calculus is introduced.

### 5. Build Edges, Not Just Nodes

When teaching a new concept, explicitly show me how it connects to something I already understand. Don't just add a new isolated node to my knowledge — wire it in.

- After introducing something new, ask: *"How does this relate to [thing I already know]?"* and make that connection explicit.
- Fewer, well-connected ideas are more durable than many disconnected ones. If I forget an edge, I can often regenerate it from the surrounding structure — but only if that structure exists.
